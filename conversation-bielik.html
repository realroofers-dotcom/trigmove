Mark Nejmeh

"Nomenklatura privatization." Okay, maybe.

Bielik

Nomenklatura privatization refers to a process where the ruling elite or government officials take control of state-owned enterprises and then privatize them, often transferring ownership to themselves or their allies. This term is particularly associated with the privatization processes in Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union during the 1990s.

In Poland, the privatization process was part of the broader transition from a centrally planned economy to a market economy after the fall of communism. The term "nomenklatura" is used to describe the group of people who benefited from these privatizations, as they had insider information and connections that allowed them to acquire state assets at low costs.

While the specific tricks and methods used in Poland may not be widely known, similar processes have occurred in other countries undergoing privatization. For example, in Russia during the 1990s, the "loan privatization" scheme allowed managers and insiders to take control of state-owned enterprises by borrowing money and using the companies as collateral. This led to the emergence of oligarchs who gained significant wealth and influence.

It's important to note that privatization processes can vary greatly depending on the political and economic context of each country. The nomenklatura privatization and similar processes have been criticized for leading to wealth concentration and corruption in some cases.

In the European Union, privatization has been a common practice, particularly in the areas of telecommunications, energy, and transportation. However, the process has generally been more transparent and regulated compared to the nomenklatura privatization in Eastern Europe. In the United States, privatization has been applied in various sectors, such as prisons, military contracting, and public utilities. What about a public American company driving the market value down, then taking a publicly traded company private and then taking it back to be publicly traded?

Bielik

A public American company can indeed drive down its market value and then be taken private, and in some cases, it may eventually go public again. Here's a general outline of the process:

  1. Driving down the market value: A company's market value can be driven down through various factors, such as poor financial performance, scandals, or market conditions. In some cases, activist investors or hedge funds may push for changes in management or strategy that could negatively impact the company's stock price.
  2. Going private: Once the market value has been reduced, a hostile or friendly takeover may occur. An investor or another company might acquire a majority stake in the company, taking it private. This is often done through a leveraged buyout (LBO), where the acquirer uses debt to finance the purchase.
  3. Restructuring and improving the company: After going private, the new owners may restructure the company, implement new strategies, or make other changes to improve its financial performance and market position.
  4. Going public again (IPO): If the company's value has been successfully increased, the owners may decide to take the company public again through an Initial Public Offering (IPO). This involves filing with the SEC, creating shareholder material, and marketing the company to potential investors.

While this process is possible, it is not uncommon for companies to stay private after being taken private. The decision to go public again depends on various factors, such as the company's performance, market conditions, and the owners' goals.

It's also important to note that going private and then back public can be a complex and time-consuming process, often involving significant financial transactions and regulatory approvals.

What if it happens from the management inside the company, using good news on advances and then warrants and shorting to drive the price down?

Bielik

The scenario you've described involves management using misleading information and financial instruments to manipulate the stock price. This is a form of market manipulation and is considered unethical and potentially illegal.

Here's a breakdown of the actions involved:

  1. Misleading information on advances: Management provides false or exaggerated information about the company's product advances to create a false sense of optimism among investors. This can lead to an initial increase in the stock price as investors become confident in the company's future prospects.
  2. Using warrants: Warrants are financial derivatives that give the holder the right to buy or sell an underlying asset at a specified price. Management might use warrants to sell the company's stock in the future at a predetermined price, which can help drive down the current stock price.
  3. Shorting the stock: Short selling is a financial strategy where an investor borrows shares of a company with the intention of selling them at a higher price and buying them back at a lower price, pocketing the difference. Management might engage in short selling to drive the stock price down.

These actions constitute market manipulation and can have serious consequences. Regulatory bodies, such as the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) in the United States, closely monitor such activities and can take legal action against those found guilty of manipulating the market.

If you suspect that market manipulation is occurring, it is essential to report it to the appropriate regulatory authorities. Additionally, investors should be cautious and conduct thorough research before investing in a company, as market manipulation can lead to significant financial losses.
